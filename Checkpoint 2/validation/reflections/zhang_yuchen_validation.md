## Prompting notes

I reviewed ChatGPT's and Claude's analysis results on the 20 student emails in our test inbox. I focused on whether they could identify which emails required action, identify deadlines, and handle tasks that required completing a prerequisite first. Both systems flagged 10 emails as “action required” and the other 10 as “passive.” They also accurately captured the condition in E09: students only need to send a reminder email if they haven’t received feedback by Friday afternoon. E01 and E06 stood out to me as particularly interesting. Claude noted that these emails might pertain to the same assignment but had inconsistent deadlines. It treated them as separate tasks and highlighted this uncertainty. I wouldn’t call this an obvious mistake. However, it made me wonder: what information do students need to see before deciding whether to combine these tasks?

## Interview notes

I spoke with two other students about how they manage their emails and whether they use tools like InboxToTask. We discussed accuracy, consistency, response time, privacy, cost, and how much control they want over the AI. Both were concerned that tasks might be overlooked or fabricated. One also noted that, in a cluttered inbox, AI might catch details humans easily overlook. However, their expectations differed. One wanted quick results—ideally within 15 seconds to 1 minute—and appreciated features like weekly task summaries and scheduling assistance. The other emphasized accuracy, didn’t mind taking longer, and wanted to cross-check the task plan against the original emails. Their privacy concerns also differed. One person felt comfortable with AI reading their emails, while the other worried about sensitive personal information leaking. They also had different views on how much the service should cost.

## One finding that changed (or confirmed) my assumption about the proposed scenario

At first, I thought the biggest benefit of InboxToTask was that it would help students save time. But after conducting the interviews, I realized I hadn’t fully considered what would happen when the AI made a mistake. Both students raised this concern, and one of them said he wished he could go back to the original email to double-check his task plan. I completely understand this. If the task list appears complete but is actually missing something, students might not even realize there’s a problem. This is closely related to the concept of “trust calibration” proposed by Gonzalez et al. (2026). Students should be able to leverage AI assistance, but they shouldn’t take it for granted that every suggestion is correct. I hope InboxToTask will display the source of each task and allow users to edit or reject it before adding it to their schedule. That way, the AI can handle the time-consuming sifting process, while students still retain the ability to decide what they truly need to do.
