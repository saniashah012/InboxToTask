# Opportunity Framing

### Hypothesis Evolution

- **Categorization:** We initially thought users needed the AI to sort emails into two categories: tasks and passive information. Interviews showed that this structure did not fit every individual workflow. Users wanted to define and customize their own categories.
- **Urgency:** We initially thought a task list sorted by deadline would be sufficient. Interviews showed that users still had to spend time interpreting dates. They wanted color coding to make approaching deadlines easier to spot at a glance.

### Prioritized Feature Matrix

| Priority | Feature | Interview Evidence | Connection to Theory |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **User-defined sorting and custom categories** | In speed-dating interviews, users rejected a fixed “task vs. information” structure and requested more control over how their emails were grouped. | Evidence that users reject rigid AI categorization shows a complementarity break where the system's logic misaligns with the user's workflow; the principle of developing **Shared Mental Models** addresses this by allowing the human to configure the AI to map accurately to their expectations. |
| **2** | **Color-coded deadline indicators** | Users said sorting by date was insufficient and requested color coding to show how close deadlines were. | Evidence that text-based dates are insufficient shows a complementarity break where the human's cognitive load remains too high to process AI output; the principle of supporting the cognitive pillar of **Attention** (via pre-attentive visual cues) addresses this by enabling rapid **Trust Calibration** without requiring deep reading. |
